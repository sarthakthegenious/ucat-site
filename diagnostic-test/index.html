VR · Trap 1 of 2 · First Attempt

Verbal Reasoning

Read the passage and answer the question. Go with your instinct.

The push towards renewable energy has accelerated in recent years. Solar installations have increased by 40% across regional Australia, and wind capacity has doubled since 2018. Proponents of renewable energy argue that fossil fuel subsidies distort the market and make clean energy appear less competitive than it actually is. The government, however, has stated that fossil fuel subsidies remain essential to stabilise energy prices for households in the short term.
QUESTION
Fossil fuel subsidies distort the energy market and make clean energy appear less competitive.
VR · Strategy Reveal

The Author's View Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
The passage says "proponents argue that fossil fuel subsidies distort the market." Most students read this and answer True — because it sounds factual and they agree with it. But the passage attributes it to proponents, not to the author as an established fact. The statement in the question is therefore unconfirmed by the passage itself.
✓ THE RULE
Before answering, ask: who said this? If the statement is attributed to a group, expert, or one side of a debate — and the author never independently confirms it — the answer is Can't Tell. You are not asked whether you believe it. You are asked whether the passage confirms it as fact.

Key attribution words to flag: "argues," "claims," "suggests," "believes," "according to," "critics say," "proponents contend," "opponents warn."
SIMPLE EXAMPLE
Passage: "Critics claim the new policy will increase unemployment."

Q: The new policy will increase unemployment. → Can't Tell. Critics claim it — the passage doesn't confirm it is true as fact.

QUESTION

VR · Strategy Reveal

The Double Negative Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
The passage opens: "Not all medical interventions that are deemed effective are without significant side effects." Unpacking that double negative is cognitively difficult under time pressure. Students either get confused and mark Can't Tell, or skim past "not" and think it says interventions are safe. The question then says "all are free of side effects" — which is directly contradicted.
✓ THE RULE — Double Negative = Positive
Any double negative always simplifies to a positive statement. Apply the rule instantly in your head:

"not all X are without Y" → some X have Y
"not uncommon" → common
"not unlike" → similar
"not without risk" → has risk
"not impossible" → possible

Double negative trigger words to watch for: "without," "unlike," "uncommon," "impossible," "unnecessary," "uncertain," "unaware" — any of these paired with a preceding "not" or "no" creates a double negative.

Once simplified: "Not all treatments are without side effects" → "Some treatments have side effects." The question claims all are side-effect free → directly False.
QUICK EXAMPLES
"Not uncommon to not disclose" → patients commonly do not disclose
"Not without consequence" → has consequences
"Not unlike previous models" → similar to previous models
Apply the rule → Spot the double negative trigger words and simplify before reading the question.

Decision Making — Syllogisms

Read the statements. Assume they are completely true. Choose the conclusion that must follow.

Statement 1: Some doctors are researchers.
Statement 2: All researchers attend academic conferences.
QUESTION — Which conclusion follows?
DM · Rules Reveal

The "Some" vs "All" Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Most students jump to answer A — "All doctors attend conferences." Their reasoning: some doctors are researchers, and all researchers attend conferences, so doctors attend conferences. But "some doctors are researchers" does not mean all doctors are. Only the doctors who are also researchers definitely attend. Treating "some" as if it means "all" is the single most common DM syllogism error.
✓ THE RULES
Rule: Some A are B + All B are C → Some A are C (never "All A are C")

Rule: All A are B + All B are C → All A are C ✓

Rule: Some A are B + Some B are C → No definite conclusion about A and C

Rule: No A are B → A is completely blocked from any categories passing through B

"Some" at any point in the chain caps the conclusion at "some" — never "all."

VISUALISE IT — VENN DIAGRAM

∴ The overlap group (some Doctors) are Researchers → and all Researchers attend Conferences → "Some Doctors" attend conferences. Not "All Doctors."

35+ SUCH RULES EXIST IN TOTAL
DM syllogisms involve over 35 distinct rules across All/Some/No combinations, Venn diagram overlaps, chain logic, and negation scenarios — each producing a different valid conclusion. These rules are covered in full in the diagnostic consultation session.
Apply the rules → Identify "some" vs "all." "Some" anywhere in the chain means the conclusion is "some" — never "all."

QR · Trap 1 of 1 · First Attempt

Quantitative Reasoning

A hospital employs 400 staff. 40% of staff are clinical. Of the clinical staff, 25% are specialists.
QUESTION
What percentage of the total hospital staff are specialists?
QR · Strategy Reveal

The Percentage of a Percentage Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Most students answer 25% — they take the last percentage mentioned and apply it directly to the total. But 25% applies to the clinical staff only (40% of all staff), not to everyone. This trap appears in almost every UCAT exam — and the key insight is that QR is less about the calculation itself and more about interpreting which calculation to actually do.
✓ THE VISUAL SHORTCUT — Think in Quarters
40% of staff are clinical → think of this as roughly 4 sections out of 10. Of those, 25% are specialists — and 25% means one quarter.

One quarter of 40% = 10% of the total. No long multiplication needed.

Alternatively: 400 staff × 40% = 160 clinical. 160 × 25% = 160 ÷ 4 = 40 specialists. 40 ÷ 400 = 10%.
Imagine the clinical staff (40%) split into 4 equal quarters.
25% of them = one quarter = 10% of all staff.

The highlighted quarter = the specialists.
No complex multiplication needed — just recognise the fraction.

4. Percentages
UCAT Complete Guidance — Wording Interpretation Summary
4a. Finding a percentage of a value
Wording What to do Example
"X% of Y", "what is X% of" (X ÷ 100) × Y "15% of 340" → 0.15 × 340 = 51
"VAT added at X%", "increased by X%" Value × (1 + X/100) "$80 VAT 20%" → 80 × 1.20 = 96
"Decreased by X%", "discount", "reduced" Value x (1 − X/100) "$200, 15% off" → 200 x0.85 = 170
"After two successive % changes" Value × multiplier1 × multiplier2 "Up 10% then down 5%" → × 1.10 × 0.95
4b. Expressing as a percentage
Wording What to do Example
"X is what % of Y" (X ÷ Y) × 100 "18 out of 60" → (18 ÷ 60) × 100 = 30%
"% change", "% increase/decrease" ((New − Old) ÷ Old) × 100 "250 → 200" → ((200−250)÷250)×100
Key rule: If the % is given in the question → use the multiplier method (×1.20 or x0.85). If asked to calculate what % something represents → use (number ÷ total) × 100. If a quantity is given and % is in the table → (quantity × %) — e.g. 300g burger × 20% fat = 60g fat.

Apply the strategy → Convert to actual numbers. Apply each % as a fraction where possible. Express the final answer as % of the original total.

Situational Judgement

Select the single most appropriate response.

A patient tells you in confidence that they have been experiencing blackouts but continue to drive. They refuse to stop driving or to inform the relevant authority. You are a medical student and there is no supervising doctor immediately available.
QUESTION — Select the MOST appropriate action
SJT · Strategy Reveal

The Confidentiality vs Public Safety Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Many students select A — confidentiality feels like the "safe" professional default. Others jump to D — immediately contacting authorities — because public safety sounds urgent. Both extremes miss the nuance. Confidentiality can be overridden when serious risk to third parties exists — but the first step is always to inform the patient and encourage voluntary disclosure, not to act unilaterally or do nothing.
✓ THE RULE — Confidentiality Override Order
Step 1: Tell the patient what may need to happen and encourage voluntary disclosure (C) — this is always first when the patient is present.

Step 2: Escalate to your supervisor (B) — but only after informing the patient first. As a student, never act alone on formal disclosure without involving your supervisor.

Step 3: Formal disclosure through proper channels follows thereafter (D).

Why not B first? Although informing your supervisor is the correct next step, doing so before speaking to the patient yourself skips the essential first step. The patient must be informed of the potential disclosure and given the opportunity to act voluntarily first. B is appropriate as a follow-up to C — but not as the very first action.

Doing nothing (A) when serious third-party risk exists is always the least appropriate option. Unilateral immediate disclosure (D) without informing the patient first bypasses the established process.
WHEN CONFIDENTIALITY CAN BE BROKEN
✓ Serious, identifiable risk to third parties (driving with blackouts, infectious disease, violence)
✓ Patient lacks capacity
✓ Court order

First step is always: inform the patient, encourage voluntary action — never unilateral external contact without this step first.
Apply the rule → Serious third-party risk overrides confidentiality. First: inform the patient and encourage voluntary disclosure. Then escalate through proper channels.
