VR · Trap 1 of 3 · First Attempt

Verbal Reasoning

Read the passage and answer the question. Go with your instinct.

The push towards renewable energy has accelerated in recent years. Solar installations have increased by 40% across regional Australia, and wind capacity has doubled since 2018. Proponents of renewable energy argue that fossil fuel subsidies distort the market and make clean energy appear less competitive than it actually is. The government, however, has stated that fossil fuel subsidies remain essential to stabilise energy prices for households in the short term.
QUESTION
Fossil fuel subsidies distort the energy market and make clean energy appear less competitive.
VR · Strategy Reveal

The Author's View Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
The passage says "proponents argue that fossil fuel subsidies distort the market." Most students read this and answer True — because it sounds factual and they agree with it. But the passage does not state this as fact. It attributes it to proponents. The author never confirms it.
✓ THE STRATEGY
Before answering, ask: who said this? If the statement in the question is attributed to a group, expert, or one side of a debate in the passage — and the author doesn't independently confirm it — the answer is Can't Tell. You are not asked if you believe it. You are asked if the passage confirms it as fact.
SIMPLE EXAMPLE
Passage: "Critics claim the new policy will increase unemployment."

Q: The new policy will increase unemployment. → Can't Tell. The critics claim it — the passage doesn't confirm it is true.

Now apply this strategy to the next question — the same trap appears, but now you know to look for who is speaking.

Apply the strategy → Watch for who is making the claim in the passage.

VR · Trap 2 of 3 · First Attempt

Verbal Reasoning

Urban green spaces have been shown to improve mental wellbeing in residents who use them regularly. A recent study found that people living within 500 metres of a park reported lower stress levels than those living further away. The study did not examine whether the proximity of parks caused residents to use them more frequently, or whether other neighbourhood factors contributed to the observed differences.
QUESTION
Living close to a park causes lower stress levels in urban residents.
VR · Strategy Reveal

The Correlation vs Causation Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
The passage says people near parks report lower stress — and it explicitly says the study did NOT examine whether proximity caused more park use or whether other factors explained it. But the question uses the word "causes." Most students mark True because the association sounds right. Correlation ≠ causation, and the passage says so directly.
✓ THE STRATEGY
Scan for causal language in the question. Words like "causes," "leads to," "results in," "due to" require the passage to confirm a causal relationship — not just an association. If the passage only shows correlation or hedges with "did not examine causation," the answer is Can't Tell.
SIMPLE EXAMPLE
Passage: "Students who eat breakfast perform better on tests."

Q: Eating breakfast causes better test performance. → Can't Tell. The passage shows association, not causation.
Apply the strategy → Look for causal language in the question, and check if the passage actually confirms cause.

VR · Trap 3 of 3 · First Attempt

Verbal Reasoning

Not all medical interventions that are deemed effective by regulatory bodies are without significant side effects. Many approved treatments carry risks that, while acknowledged in clinical literature, are not consistently communicated to patients during consultations. Patient advocacy groups have called for mandatory disclosure frameworks, arguing that informed consent cannot be meaningful without complete risk communication.
QUESTION
All medical interventions approved by regulatory bodies are free of significant side effects.
VR · Strategy Reveal

The "All / Not All" Negation Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
The passage opens: "Not all medical interventions that are deemed effective are without significant side effects." Unpacking that double negative is cognitively difficult under time pressure. Students either get confused and mark Can't Tell, or skim past "not" and think it says interventions are safe. The question then says "all... are free of side effects" — which is directly contradicted.
✓ THE STRATEGY
Rewrite double negatives in plain language before reading the question. "Not all X are without Y" = "Some X have Y." Write it simply: "Some approved treatments have side effects." Now the question ("All approved treatments are free of side effects") is clearly False. One second to rewrite saves the question.
SIMPLE EXAMPLE
Passage: "Not all students who fail are unprepared." → Rewrite: "Some students who fail are prepared."

Q: All students who fail are unprepared. → False. The passage directly contradicts this.
Apply the strategy → Rewrite any double negative in the passage into plain language before reading the question.

DM · Trap 1 of 3 · First Attempt

Decision Making — Syllogisms

Read the statements carefully. Assume they are true.

Statement 1: Some doctors are researchers.
Statement 2: All researchers attend academic conferences.
QUESTION
Which conclusion follows?
DM · Strategy Reveal

The "Some" vs "All" Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Most students jump to answer A — "All doctors attend conferences." Their reasoning: some doctors are researchers, and all researchers attend conferences, so doctors attend conferences. But "some doctors are researchers" does not mean all doctors are. Only the doctors who are also researchers definitely attend conferences.
✓ THE STRATEGY — Draw a Venn Diagram mentally
"Some" means overlap — not total inclusion. Draw a quick circle for Doctors and a circle for Researchers with partial overlap. Only the overlapping section definitely attends conferences. So: Some doctors (those who are researchers) attend conferences. "All doctors" is never justified from "some."
VENN RULE TO MEMORISE
Some A are B + All B are C → Some A are C. Never "All A are C."

Some A are B + Some B are C → No definite conclusion about A and C.

All A are B + All B are C → All A are C. ✓
Apply the strategy → Identify the "some" vs "all" language before concluding. Draw a quick mental Venn.

DM · Trap 2 of 3 · First Attempt

Decision Making — Logical Puzzles

Four students — Alex, Ben, Cara, and Dana — each received either a Distinction or a Pass in their exam. Exactly two students received a Distinction. Alex and Ben did not both receive the same grade. Cara received a Distinction.
QUESTION
Which of the following cannot be true?
DM · Strategy Reveal

The "Cannot Be True" Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Students get confused between "must be true," "could be true," and "cannot be true." On "cannot be true" questions, they often select answers that could be false — not ones that are impossible. Here: Alex and Ben cannot both have Distinction because the clue explicitly states they did not get the same grade. That makes D impossible, not just unlikely.
✓ THE STRATEGY — Test each answer
For "cannot be true" questions: try to make each answer work. If you can construct a valid scenario where the answer is true, it's not the answer. The correct answer is the one where no valid scenario exists.

Work from constraints outward: what is fixed? Cara = Distinction. Exactly 2 Distinctions total. Alex ≠ Ben grade. → Only one of Alex/Ben can have Distinction. So both having it is impossible.
THE 3-QUESTION METHOD
For each answer option ask:
1. What is locked in by the constraints?
2. Can I build a valid scenario where this is true?
3. If yes → eliminate. If no scenario works → that's the answer.
Apply the strategy → Lock in the constraints first, then test each option by asking: can I build a valid scenario where this is true?

DM · Trap 3 of 3 · First Attempt

Decision Making — Venn Diagrams

Statement 1: No surgeons are general practitioners.
Statement 2: Some general practitioners are rural doctors.
Statement 3: All rural doctors work long hours.
QUESTION — Which conclusion definitely follows?
DM · Strategy Reveal

The Chain + "No" Statement Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Students see "No surgeons are GPs" and "All rural doctors work long hours" and try to connect surgeons to long hours. But the chain only runs through GPs → rural doctors → long hours. Surgeons are completely excluded from GPs, so no conclusion about surgeons and long hours is possible. Others mark D ("All GPs work long hours") ignoring that only some GPs are rural doctors.
✓ THE STRATEGY — Follow the chain strictly
Write the chain: Some GPs → rural doctors → long hours. "Some" at any link in the chain means the conclusion is "some," never "all." A "No" statement breaks any chain — if no A are B, then A cannot reach any conclusion through B. Trace the chain and stop where it breaks.
CHAIN RULE
No A → B (blocked)
Some B → C (some overlap)
All C → D (full inclusion)

Conclusion: Some B are D. A is completely excluded. Never draw A into any conclusion that runs through B.
Apply the strategy → Trace the chain step by step. Stop where a "No" blocks the connection. "Some" anywhere in the chain caps your conclusion at "some."

QR · Trap 1 of 3 · First Attempt

Quantitative Reasoning

A hospital employs 400 staff. 60% of staff are clinical. Of the clinical staff, 25% are specialists.
QUESTION
What percentage of the total hospital staff are specialists?
QR · Strategy Reveal

The Percentage of a Percentage Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Most students answer 25% — they take the last percentage mentioned and apply it directly to the total. But 25% is of the clinical staff, not of all staff. The question asks for specialists as a percentage of everyone. You must multiply the percentages: 60% × 25% = 15%. This trap appears in almost every UCAT exam.
✓ THE STRATEGY
Whenever you see a percentage of a subgroup, always check what the final question is asking the percentage of. If the question asks for a % of the whole, multiply the chain of percentages together.

60% × 25% = 0.6 × 0.25 = 0.15 = 15%

Or use actual numbers: 400 × 60% = 240 clinical. 240 × 25% = 60 specialists. 60/400 = 15%.
QUICK CHECK METHOD
Step 1: Convert to actual numbers using the total.
Step 2: Apply each percentage in sequence.
Step 3: Express the final number as a % of the original total.

This takes 15 seconds and eliminates the trap entirely.
Apply the strategy → Convert to actual numbers first. Apply each % in sequence. Express the final answer as % of the original total.

QR · Trap 2 of 3 · First Attempt

Quantitative Reasoning

Seven patients waited the following times (in minutes) to be seen in an emergency department:

8, 12, 10, 9, 11, 14, 95
QUESTION
A hospital report states that the "average" waiting time best represents typical patient experience. Which measure should they use, and what is its value?
QR · Strategy Reveal

The Mean vs Median Outlier Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Students default to calculating the mean because "average" = mean in everyday language. But the mean here is pulled to ~22.7 by the outlier (95 min) — far above every other data point. This does not represent "typical" experience. The question is actually testing whether you recognise when the mean is misleading.
✓ THE STRATEGY
When you see a dataset, scan for outliers before calculating. If one value is dramatically higher or lower than the rest, the median is almost always the better measure of typical experience. Sorted: 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 95. The middle value (4th of 7) = 11. This accurately represents the typical patient.

In UCAT QR, if a question asks which measure "best represents" or is "most appropriate" for a skewed dataset — median wins whenever an outlier is visible.
QUICK SCAN RULE
Before calculating mean: look at the spread. If one number is 3× or more the others → outlier present → median is the better central tendency → sort and find the middle value.
Apply the strategy → Scan for outliers before calculating. If an outlier exists, use median — sort and find the middle value.

QUESTION
How many patients does the North region see per month?
QR · Strategy Reveal

The Unit / Column Header Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
The column says "Patients/month (hundreds)" — meaning each number must be multiplied by 100. Students read 12 from the table and answer 12, or sometimes 120 (multiplying by 10 instead of 100). Under time pressure, column headers are skimmed or misread. This is one of the most common QR errors in real UCAT exams.
✓ THE STRATEGY
Before reading any number from a table, read every column header completely — including the unit in brackets. Underline or mentally flag unit modifiers: "(hundreds)", "(thousands)", "($000s)", "(per 1000 population)". Apply the multiplier before doing any arithmetic.

12 hundreds = 12 × 100 = 1,200 patients.
30-SECOND TABLE RULE
When a table appears: spend 10 seconds reading ALL column headers before touching the numbers. Identify any unit multipliers. Only then extract values. This prevents the single most common QR error.
Apply the strategy → Read every column header before reading numbers. Apply the unit multiplier before calculating.

SJT · Trap 1 of 3 · First Attempt

Situational Judgement

Rank all four options from most (1) to least (4) appropriate. Click in order of your preference.

You are a medical student on a hospital placement. You notice that a senior nurse has prepared the wrong dose of a medication for a patient. The nurse is about to administer it. You are not sure if your observation is correct.
RANK from Most Appropriate (1) to Least Appropriate (4)
SJT · Strategy Reveal

The Hierarchy vs Patient Safety Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Many students rank C (go straight to the doctor) as most appropriate because it sounds safe and decisive. Others rank A (say nothing) to avoid conflict with a senior. Both are wrong. Patient safety always overrides hierarchy — but the first escalation step is always the most direct and immediate one: speak to the person administering before escalating past them.
✓ THE STRATEGY — The Escalation Ladder
Step 1: Address directly at the source first — politely raise concern with the nurse immediately. Step 2: Escalate if step 1 fails or is impossible. Skipping step 1 and going straight to the doctor is premature escalation — appropriate only if the person is unavailable or unresponsive.

Doing nothing (A) is always ranked last when patient safety is at stake. Asking a peer (D) delays appropriate action.
THE SJT ESCALATION RULE
1. Raise concern directly with the person involved
2. Escalate to the next level if unresolved
3. Document and report through formal channels
4. Do NOT do nothing when patient safety is at immediate risk
Apply the strategy → Patient safety first. Address directly at source before escalating past the person involved.

SJT · Trap 2 of 3 · First Attempt

Situational Judgement

A 45-year-old patient with capacity has been informed of all risks and benefits of a recommended surgical procedure. They have decided to decline the surgery, preferring to manage their condition conservatively, despite their surgeon's strong recommendation to proceed. The patient is not in immediate danger.
RANK from Most Appropriate (1) to Least Appropriate (4)
SJT · Strategy Reveal

The Patient Autonomy Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Students who think "the doctor knows best" rank B or C highly — continuing to push the patient, or getting another surgeon to reinforce the pressure. But a patient with capacity has an absolute right to decline treatment, even if the decision seems unwise clinically. Persisting after an informed refusal is a violation of autonomy, not good practice.
✓ THE STRATEGY — The Capacity Rule
If the patient has capacity + has been fully informed → their decision must be respected. Full stop. The appropriate response is:
1. Respect and document the decision (A)
2. Offer alternative management within their chosen framework (D)
3. Getting a second surgeon to pressure them (C) is inappropriate
4. Continued pressure after an informed decision (B) is always ranked last
CAPACITY CHECKLIST
Before ranking — ask: Does the patient have capacity? Have they been fully informed? If YES to both: their choice is final. The appropriate actions are respecting, documenting, and offering alternatives within their chosen path.
Apply the strategy → Capacity + fully informed = patient's decision is final. Respect it, document it, offer alternatives within their framework.

SJT · Trap 3 of 3 · First Attempt

Situational Judgement

A patient tells you in confidence that they have been experiencing blackouts but continue to drive. They refuse to stop driving or to inform the DVLA. You are a medical student and there is no supervising doctor immediately available.
RANK from Most Appropriate (1) to Least Appropriate (4)
SJT · Strategy Reveal

The Confidentiality vs Public Safety Trap

⚠ THE TRAP
Many students rank A first — confidentiality feels like the "safe" professional answer. Others jump to D (call the DVLA immediately) because public safety sounds urgent. Both extremes miss the nuance: confidentiality can be broken when there is serious risk to third parties, but the first step is always to inform the patient and encourage voluntary disclosure — not to act unilaterally or do nothing.
✓ THE STRATEGY — The Confidentiality Override Rule
Confidentiality is not absolute. When a patient poses serious risk to identifiable others (driving with blackouts, infectious disease, violence):

1. Tell the patient what may need to happen and encourage them to disclose voluntarily (C)
2. Escalate to supervisor — as a student you must not act alone on disclosure (B)
3. Formal disclosure to authorities follows through proper channels
4. Doing nothing (A) when serious third-party risk exists is always inappropriate
CONFIDENTIALITY EXCEPTION RULE
Confidentiality CAN be broken: serious risk to identifiable third parties, patient lacks capacity, court order.
First step: always inform the patient and encourage voluntary disclosure before any external reporting.
Apply the strategy → Serious risk to third parties overrides confidentiality. First: inform patient and encourage voluntary disclosure. Then escalate through proper channels.
